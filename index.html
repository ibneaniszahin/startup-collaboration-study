Bangladesh’s startup problem isn’t only capital. It’s that the agencies meant to help struggle to work with each other.

A mixed-methods study of collaboration among the government agencies behind Bangladesh’s startup ecosystem — 15 organizations, interviews, document analysis, and a collaboration map built with USAID’s mapping instrument. This page presents the study’s findings in a form you can explore in minutes.

01The paradox

Governance challenges arise not only from institutional gaps — but from coordination failures inside the public sector itself.

Bangladesh’s startup ecosystem is real and growing: over $1bn invested across 479 deals as of April 2025, more than 2,500 active startups, and a government that funds them directly through Startup Bangladesh Ltd (SBL), the country’s first state-sponsored venture capital firm. Yet entrepreneurs still report paying up to six times the official fees to obtain licenses from different agencies, and the country ranks 149 of 180 on the Corruption Perceptions Index.

The study asks a question most of the literature skips — not how government partners with the private sector, but how government agencies collaborate with each other. The answer: hierarchical communication structures, collectivist organizational norms, fragmented institutional mandates, and limited startup literacy among policymakers hinder information sharing and inter-agency coordination — and there is no horizontal collaboration framework to counteract any of it.

THE INSTRUMENT

One survey, three dimensions, fourteen answers

Each organization rated its relationship with SBL on three 1–10 scales: relationship strength (distance from centre), knowledge about startups (circle size), and information flow (fill darkness). The qualitative phase — 11 interviews and document analysis — guided how the map is read.

Redrawn from the paper’s Figure 3. Positions and shades approximate the published figure; the per-organization scores were not published.

VISIBLE TREND 1 / 4

The private sector sits closest — on all three dimensions

VCs, financiers and consultancies cluster near SBL: strong relationships, high startup literacy, dark fills. The paper’s explanation is structural — SBL is largely staffed by private-sector employees, so it speaks their language fluently.

The same fact cuts the other way: staff without government experience may struggle to navigate ministries run by career bureaucrats.

VISIBLE TREND 2 / 4

Funding creates its own gravity

Startups that received SBL funding — iFarmer is the paper’s example — show stronger relationships and better information flow than startups that did not. And startups, unsurprisingly, know startups: their circles are large.

VISIBLE TREND 3 / 4

The core finding: government agencies are far, and the flow is thin

Some agencies are quite knowledgeable about startups — yet their relationship with SBL and the flow of information remain low. A government VC firm exchanges information more easily with private financiers than with its own peer agencies.

This is the coordination failure, visible: knowledge exists at the nodes; it doesn’t move between them.

VISIBLE TREND 4 / 4

Academia holds the middle

The two universities score moderately on all three dimensions — connected enough to matter, not yet central to how the ecosystem coordinates.

THE PATTERN

Read together: the funding flows; the information doesn’t

Private sector close and dark. Funded startups pulled inward. Government agencies distant and light. The map shows support existing at every node — and coordination failing between precisely the nodes that share a government.

02Findings · what produces the map

Four factors, named by the findings

The abstract states them plainly. The interviews and document analysis show how they interlock — and why no single agency can fix them alone.

1 FACTOR 1

Hierarchical communication

Top-down structures dominate; information favours vertical channels. Lower-level employees are often reluctant to challenge authority, so upward communication is limited — and information hoarding becomes more likely.

2 FACTOR 2

Collectivist norms

Consensus-building, harmony and conflict avoidance govern how sensitive issues are raised — or not raised. High-context, indirect communication further discourages open exchange between agencies.

3 FACTOR 3

Fragmented mandates

Responsibilities are split across SBL, BIDA, BSEC, RJSC, BB and NBR with no shared stakeholder list and no horizontal collaboration framework. Agencies operate in silos; policies emerge fragmented.

4 FACTOR 4

Limited startup literacy

Agencies are familiar with the vocabulary — funding, valuation, exits, IP, pilot licensing — but the map shows practical understanding remains limited. This gap hinders policy design and cooperation alike.

A note on Singapore

Not the subject — the reference point

This is a study of Bangladesh. Singapore enters it only where the paper itself places it, in two supporting roles:

In the framing · Schein (1996)

The literature’s benchmark

The study cites Edgar Schein’s case study of Singapore’s Economic Development Board, which identified sectoral collaboration as a key cultural paradigm behind the EDB’s success — and then turns Schein’s own levels-of-culture framework on Bangladesh’s agencies. The benchmark and the diagnosis share one methodology.

In the findings · §5.3

The founders’ destination

Many Bangladeshi startups continue to register in Singapore, citing stringent domestic requirements and limited funding policies. Where companies choose to incorporate is a revealed-preference reading of the coordination gap the map documents.

The counter-efforts are in the record too. Bangladesh Bank has introduced Startup Financing Directives, a Share Swap Framework and a Fund of Funds — yet clear tax incentives and a regulatory sandbox remain missing, and the paper’s point is that these gaps are interconnected: no single agency can close them alone.

03Where the paper points

From diagnosis to design

The study closes with a proposal, not just a problem: a direct-link horizontal collaboration network, with SBL and BIDA as the principal coordinating entities — BIDA because it sits under the Prime Minister’s Office and already runs cross-agency machinery through the One Stop Service Act. A structured platform of that kind targets exactly what the map shows missing: lateral channels between agencies.

04Lessons for emerging economies

The lesson travels.

In emerging economies, government policy looms larger than in advanced ones — but the study’s findings suggest its effectiveness depends not only on the presence of supportive initiatives, but on the ability of government agencies to coordinate their efforts.

Internal governance barriers

Fragmented responsibilities · limited information flow · hierarchical culture — operating inside the broader constraints of institutional gaps and scarce resources.

Public sector coordination failure
The answer: a collaboration hub

Specialized government entities like SBL can align policy priorities, facilitate multi-level stakeholder interaction, address ecosystem bottlenecks and bridge contextual differences — cultural norms, administrative structure.

05The author’s extension

Notes for an investment reader

The paper is public-administration research; it does not give investment advice. These four notes are the author’s extension — what the findings imply when read from an allocator’s seat.

You can screen for coordination quality

The study shows governance failure can originate inside the public sector even where support programs exist. For ecosystem-level exposure in emerging markets, the presence of a horizontal coordination framework predicts policy delivery better than the count of support initiatives.

Where companies register is a live indicator

The Singapore registration flow documented in the findings is revealed preference: companies pricing the coordination gap and paying the moving cost. An outbound registration flow is an early institutional-quality signal, visible long before macro data moves.

Value creation and value capture are decoupling

Companies that operate in Bangladesh increasingly have their legal home in Singapore. For anyone assessing an investment, that split matters: value is created in one country and owned in another — and everything in between is where the risk hides.

Watch for mechanism change, not budget change

If the real constraint is coordination, then the signals worth watching are structural changes, not spending announcements: the proposed SBL–BIDA coordination platform, a regulatory sandbox, clear tax incentives. New funds without new mechanisms leave the constraint untouched.

What to take with you
For policymakers

Coordination is buildable. A hub with a cross-agency mandate — the SBL–BIDA design above — targets the missing lateral channels directly. Watch for mechanism change, not budget change.

For investors

Coordination quality is something you can screen for, and registration flow is its live indicator. Where a market’s best companies incorporate is a continuously updated reading of institutional quality.

For researchers

The diagnostic is public and replicable: the USAID instrument above, qualitatively anchored, with Bangladesh as the reference implementation. To map your own ecosystem the same way — or compare notes — write to zahin.ibneanis@gmail.com.
